Business Marathon · E-Summit '19, RVCE Entrepreneurship Cell

Selling cement to a room of engineers

Every other thing in this archive was judged on whether it ran. This one was judged on whether anyone would pay for it, which turned out to be a much harder question to answer at four in the morning.

Nishanth Aluhonnu InterruptZero · 23 Feb 2019 · 1st Position · 4 min read

E-Summit '19 ran at RVCE from the 21st to the 23rd of February, under the banner "Breaking Barriers". It was the Entrepreneurship Cell's flagship, and it was mostly not for people like me: a stock market workshop, a mock IPL auction, design thinking with IBM, an open mic. Buried in the programme was an overnight event called Business Marathon, teams of five or six, prizes worth around ₹80,000.

Six of us entered: Rajat Keshri, Padmapriya M, Shreekara S, Saketh S, Snighdha P and me. We were Team 04.

01A different sport

I had done three hackathons by this point, and the reflexes they build are all wrong for this.

At a hackathon the deliverable is a thing that runs. You can be behind at midnight and ahead by six, because the gap between nothing and something is one working demo. Judges forgive a lot when the screen does what you said it would.

A business plan competition has no screen to point at. The deliverable is an argument. Nothing compiles, nothing fails a test, and there is no moment where the thing suddenly works and you know you are safe. You just keep making the argument better until they stop you.

02The brief, and what we did with it

The brief was to work up an approach to an environmental problem. That is wide enough to be genuinely difficult, because the easy answers are all taken and all obviously unfunded.

We went at cement.

Cement is one of those problems that hides in plain sight. It is the most used manufactured substance on earth, and making it is chemically committed to releasing carbon dioxide: you get it by cooking limestone until the carbonate breaks apart, and the CO2 comes off the rock itself, before you have burned anything to heat the kiln. You cannot efficiency your way out of that part. It is the reaction.

Our proposal was a fibre-glass cement made using recycled glass in place of conventional concrete cement, pitched on two claims: it diverts glass from landfill, and the result is stronger than what it replaces.

To be straight about it: that was a student business plan, not a validated material. We did not run compression tests, we did not cost a plant, and nothing in the surviving record backs up the strength claim with a number. What we had was a market, a waste stream and an argument. The judging was on the argument.

03What the room actually wanted

The useful lesson was about which questions carry weight in front of that panel, and they were not the questions I expected.

Nobody asked how the material worked. They asked who buys it first, and why that buyer switches from something that already works and that their supply chain is already built around. They asked what the thing costs against the incumbent, because in construction, price is close to the whole decision. They asked how you get anyone to care, which is how we ended up with most of a night spent on marketing and awareness rather than on the product.

An engineer's instinct is to make the thing better. The room wanted to know who would notice.

That reframing is the part I kept. It is the same reflex that makes you ask what a benchmark is being compared against, or who reads the output of a system before you decide how it should be shaped, and it has been more useful to me since than anything I learned about cement.

04Result

First place: ₹12,000 in cash and gift vouchers worth ₹20,000, from about 45 teams.

There is no repository for this one and there never was. It is the only entry in this archive where the deliverable was a sentence, and the only one where I could not tell you, afterwards, whether the idea was any good. We won the argument. That is a different claim from being right.
